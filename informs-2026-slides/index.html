Motivation

How do we derive scores from comparisons?

Like Elo ratings in chess: one rating per player, from many games against different opponents. The canonical model: Bradley–Terry.
Model
$$\mathbb{P}(i \text{ beats } j) = \sigma(s_i - s_j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}}$$
$s_i$: latent score of item $i$
Estimation: maximum likelihood
$$\hat{s} \in \arg\max_{s} \sum_{\text{comparisons } i \succ j} \log \sigma(s_i - s_j)$$
Estimated scores
Motivation

Advantages of Bradley–Terry

Item F appears in 6 comparisons.
Many comparisons per item
As a consequence, we can:
1
Tolerate noise. It is a statistical model: errors in individual LLM answers average out.
2
Correct biases. E.g. position bias: the LLM tends to favor the item shown first. Add one parameter $\beta$:
$$\mathbb{P}(i \text{ beats } j \mid i \text{ shown first}) = \sigma(s_i - s_j + \beta)$$
$\beta$ is estimated by maximum likelihood, together with the scores.

Our approach & theory

A model of the LLM’s two decisions

1Does the LLM cite feature $k$ when comparing $i$ and $j$?
$$\mathbb{P}(\text{cite } k \mid i, j) = \sigma\big(\,|S_{ik} - S_{jk}| + \gamma_k\,\big)$$
$S_{ik}$: intrinsic score of item $i$ on feature $k$ (unknown)
$\gamma_k$: citation bias
2If $k$ is cited, which item is stronger?
$$\mathbb{P}(i \text{ stronger on } k \mid i, j,\ k \text{ cited}) = \sigma\big(\,S_{ik} - S_{jk} + \beta\,\big)$$
$\beta$: position bias ($i$ shown first)
Our approach & theory

Step 3: estimate by maximum likelihood

Combining both decisions, we get our LLM choice model. Verdict $T_{ijk} \in \{+1, -1, 0\}$ ($i$ stronger, $j$ stronger, not cited):
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(T_{ijk} = +1) &= \sigma\big(|S_{ik} - S_{jk}| + \gamma_k\big)\;\sigma\big(S_{ik} - S_{jk} + \beta\big)\\ \mathbb{P}(T_{ijk} = -1) &= \sigma\big(|S_{ik} - S_{jk}| + \gamma_k\big)\;\big(1 - \sigma(S_{ik} - S_{jk} + \beta)\big)\\ \mathbb{P}(T_{ijk} = 0) &= 1 - \sigma\big(|S_{ik} - S_{jk}| + \gamma_k\big) \end{aligned}$$
$$(\hat S, \hat\gamma, \hat\beta) \in \arg\max_{S,\,\gamma,\,\beta} \;\sum_{\text{pairs } (i,j)} \;\sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \mathbb{P}\big(T_{ijk}\big) \;-\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\|S\|_F^2$$
Our approach & theory

Step 4: drop unreliable features

✕ dropped
Every score has a confidence interval (asymptotic normality of the MLE). More comparisons, narrower intervals.
Reliability of feature $k$
$$\rho_k = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat S_{\cdot k}) - \nu_k}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat S_{\cdot k})}$$
$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat S_{\cdot k})$: spread of the scores across items (the dots).
$\nu_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(\hat S_{ik})$: mean estimation variance (error bars).
Drop feature $k$ if $\rho_k < \rho_{\min}$ (fixed in advance), then refit.
Our approach & theory

Scoring new items on the same scale

1
Fix the scores of the existing items: they define the scoring methodology.
2
Compare the new item with a few existing items, on each feature.
3
Estimate only the new item’s scores: same features, same scale.

Recap: advantages of this approach

1

2

Our approach & theory

The estimator is well behaved

Not a standard problem: the likelihood is non-convex, and the number of parameters grows with the number of items $n$.
Theorem (informal)
One feature; every pair compared at least once and a bounded number of times. With high probability, the likelihood has a local maximizer $\hat{s}$, unique near the truth, such that
$\displaystyle \|\hat{s} - s^*\|_\infty \;\le\; C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}$
Every item’s score is consistent at once; the bias parameters $\gamma, \beta$ converge at the same rate.
